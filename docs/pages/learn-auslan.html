About Auslan

  • Auslan is the language of the Australian Deaf community, with its own grammar. It isn't signed English.
  • It shares its two-handed alphabet with British and New Zealand Sign Language. American Sign Language is a different language with a one-handed alphabet.
  • 16,242 people said they use Auslan at home in the 2021 Census, up from 5,306 in 2001.
  • Your face is part of the grammar. Eyebrows, mouth and head movements change what a sign means.

The alphabet

Your other hand is the page; the hand you write with is the pen. Use fingerspelling for names, places and words without a sign.

Vowels live on your fingertips

Point with your dominant index finger to the tips of your other hand, starting at the thumb: A, E, I, O, U.

All 26 letters

Tap a letter for step-by-step instructions. A dot means you've opened it.

Diagrams are simplified. Watch the letters move on Auslan Signbank's fingerspelling page, which can play them slow, medium or fast.

Numbers 0 to 10

Numbers use one hand. For one to five, your palm faces you.

The six-to-nine pattern

Six is a raised thumb. Turn your fist sideways and add one finger for each number after that: seven, eight, nine.

Numbers vary between regions, especially above ten. See every number from ten to a billion on Auslan Signbank's number signs.

First signs

Signs move, so each one links to a video. Read the steps, watch the video, then copy it in a mirror.

Sign descriptions are summarised in our own words from Sign Planet. Regional variants exist; Auslan Signbank shows which states use each one.

Practise

Short rounds work best. Reading fingerspelling is harder than doing it, so give it extra time.

Deaf culture

Learning Auslan means joining a community, not just learning hand shapes.

Good manners

  • To get someone's attention, tap their shoulder or arm, wave where they can see you, or flick the lights.
  • Keep eye contact while you're signing or being signed to. Looking away reads as not paying attention.
  • Ask how a person prefers to communicate. Not every deaf person signs; some lipread or speak, and writing it down is fine.
  • When working with an interpreter, look at and speak to the Deaf person, not the interpreter.
  • Fingerspell your name when you introduce yourself. Sign names are given to you by Deaf people over time.
  • Don't shout, cover your mouth, or talk while chewing if someone is lipreading you.
  • Don't treat Auslan as a party trick. Learn it from Deaf teachers if you can.

Worth knowing

  • Deaf and deaf. A capital D usually means a person who belongs to the Deaf community and uses Auslan. Lowercase deaf describes hearing loss.
  • Recognition. The Australian Government recognised Auslan as a community language in its 1987 national language policy, and again in 1991.
  • Two main dialects. A northern dialect (NSW, Queensland, ACT) and a southern one (Victoria, SA, WA, Tasmania, NT). Colours and some numbers differ.
  • Family tree. Auslan grew mainly from British Sign Language brought by Deaf immigrants, and is close to BSL and NZSL.
  • Faces are grammar. Questions like what, where and who usually come with furrowed brows; yes/no questions with raised brows.
  • Peak body. Deaf Australia represents the Auslan-using Deaf community nationally.

Where to take a class

TAFEs and community colleges run Auslan courses, from short evening classes to a Certificate II, III and IV and a Diploma. Look for courses taught by Deaf teachers.
